# Unit 3.1 — Doubles & Halves

- **Unit:** 3.1
- **Domain:** D3 — Addition & Subtraction Strategies
- **Purpose:** build doubles as a *structural* anchor (two equal parts of a known whole)
  rather than a rote fact list, use near-doubles to derive nearby facts, and introduce
  halving as doubling's inverse — including the honest case where halving doesn't split
  evenly.
- **Prerequisites:** Unit 1.1 (part/whole, bonds), Unit 2.1 (place value, for the
  two-digit transfer lesson).
- **Unlocks:** Unit 3.2 (Compensation, which often uses a "near-double" landing point);
  D5 (Multiplicative Reasoning) — doubling is repeated addition by 2, the seed of
  multiplication.
- **Primary patterns:** Double/Half, Part/Whole.
- **Primary representations:** `NumberBond` (equal parts), `NumberLine`.
- **Lesson count:** 7 instructional lessons (+ 1 unit mastery check).

---

### 3.1.1 — Doubles I Can See
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry
- **Teaching goal:** a "double" is a whole made of two *equal* parts — a special case of
  the part/whole bonds already known.
- **Student question:** "What makes this bond special?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1 (bonds).
- **New idea:** doubles as bonds with equal parts, not a separately memorized category.
- **Reuses:** `NumberBond` (1.1), part/whole.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → `NumberBond` (both parts visibly equal).
- **Vocabulary:** double, equal parts.
- **SETUP:** two equal groups of 6 counters each, side by side.
- **SEE:** "What do you notice about these two groups?"
- **BREAK:** confirm the two groups are exactly equal (pairing, 0.1.4's tool).
- **BUILD:** combine them and name the whole: 6 + 6 = 12.
- **TRANSFORM:** "6 and 6" (an ordinary bond) named specially as "double 6" — same value,
  a special-case name for a familiar form.
- **CHECK:** re-split the 12 back into two equal groups to confirm they're still 6 and 6.
- **Primary example:** 6 + 6 = 12.
- **Second example:** 4 + 4 = 8.
- **Transfer example:** 8 + 8, without objects — reasoned from the pattern.
- **Misconception:** treats "double" as meaning "add 2" rather than "add the same
  amount again." *Why:* the everyday word "double" can be confused with counting by twos
  — worth explicitly distinguishing from the start.
- **Teacher response:** "Doubling 6 means 6 and 6 together — how is that different from
  6 and 2?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (re-split to confirm equal parts).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly identifies and states 2 doubles facts from equal
  object groups.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.2.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 3.1.2 — Doubles Facts to 20
- **Type / Difficulty:** Fluency · Developing
- **Teaching goal:** retrieve doubles facts 1+1 through 10+10 quickly, after the concept
  (3.1.1) is secure.
- **Student question:** "Can you answer without building it this time?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.1 secure.
- **New idea:** none — a deliberate Fluency lesson (§7), following the same rationale as
  Unit 1.2.4.
- **Reuses:** 3.1.1 entirely.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half.
- **Representation:** Symbol only, with `NumberBond` available as fallback.
- **Vocabulary:** (none new).
- **SETUP:** a run of doubles prompts, 1+1 through 10+10, in mixed order.
- **SEE:** "What's double this number?"
- **BREAK:** retrieve from memory; fall back to bond-building only if stuck.
- **BUILD:** answer, then move to the next.
- **TRANSFORM:** — (drilling an established relationship, not introducing a new one).
- **CHECK:** self-check with objects for any hesitant answer.
- **Primary example:** the full run, self-paced.
- **Second example:** the same run, repeated for improvement.
- **Transfer example:** doubles asked in a real-context wrapper ("if you have 7 stickers
  and get the same number again, how many now?").
- **Misconception:** confuses a double with the *next* number in the counting sequence
  (e.g., answers "double 6 is 7"). *Why:* conflating "one more" with "twice as many" —
  flagged in `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`.
- **Teacher response:** "Is doubling the same as counting one more, or adding the same
  amount again?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (bond, used sparingly).
- **Mastery evidence:** answers at least 8 of 10 doubles facts (1–10) correctly within a
  few seconds each.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.3; fluency here directly speeds every remaining lesson in this unit.
- **Visual:** symbol-only prompt; `NumberBond` fallback.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Not needed.

### 3.1.3 — Near-Doubles: One More Than a Double
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Developing/Secure
- **Teaching goal:** derive a fact one more than a known double by adding 1 to the
  double's answer (e.g., 6 + 7 = 6 + 6 + 1).
- **Student question:** "Is there a double hiding close to this problem?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.2 (doubles fluent).
- **New idea:** using a known double as a stepping stone to a *nearby*, unmemorized fact
  — an instance of Make a Benchmark using doubles as the benchmark instead of 5/10.
- **Reuses:** doubles facts (3.1.2).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Make a Benchmark.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` / `EquationWorkspace`.
- **Vocabulary:** near-double, one more.
- **SETUP:** `6 + 7`, unsolved.
- **SEE:** "Is one of these numbers part of a double you already know?"
- **BREAK:** rewrite 7 as `6 + 1` (recognizing 6+7 as "double 6, plus one more").
- **BUILD:** `6 + 6 = 12` (the known double).
- **TRANSFORM:** `12 + 1 = 13` — same value as `6 + 7`, reached via a known double.
- **CHECK:** `7 + 6 = 13` (commutativity).
- **Primary example:** `6 + 7 = 13`.
- **Second example:** `4 + 5 = 9`.
- **Transfer example:** `8 + 9`.
- **Misconception:** adds 1 to the *wrong* side (e.g., treats 6+7 as "double 7 minus 1"
  and then still adds instead of subtracts). *Why:* has correctly spotted a nearby
  double but not tracked whether the target is one more or one less than it — the exact
  distinction 3.1.4 will drill deliberately.
- **Teacher response:** "Is 7 one more than 6, or one less? Does the double need a plus
  or a minus?"
- **Check strategy:** commutativity.
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly derives 2 near-double-plus-one facts from a known
  double.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.4.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`, `EquationWorkspace`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 3.1.4 — Near-Doubles: One Less Than a Double
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** derive a fact one *less* than a known double (e.g., 6 + 5 = 6 + 6 −
  1), completing the pair with 3.1.3 and forcing the plus/minus distinction.
- **Student question:** "This time, is the answer more or less than the double?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.3.
- **New idea:** none conceptually new — the deliberate mirror case of 3.1.3, designed to
  directly confront that lesson's flagged misconception.
- **Reuses:** 3.1.3's method, in reverse.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Make a Benchmark.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` / `EquationWorkspace`.
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce near-double, one less).
- **SETUP:** `6 + 5`, presented immediately after a `6 + 7`-style problem for contrast.
- **SEE:** "Is 5 one more than 6, or one less?"
- **BREAK:** rewrite 5 as `6 - 1` (recognizing 6+5 as "double 6, minus one").
- **BUILD:** `6 + 6 = 12` (the known double).
- **TRANSFORM:** `12 - 1 = 11` — same value as `6 + 5`, reached via a known double.
- **CHECK:** `5 + 6 = 11` (commutativity).
- **Primary example:** `6 + 5 = 11`.
- **Second example:** `4 + 3 = 7`.
- **Transfer example:** given both `8 + 9` and `8 + 7` together, correctly apply +1 to
  one and −1 to the other.
- **Misconception:** applies the same "+1" rule from 3.1.3 regardless of whether the
  actual case needs +1 or −1. *Why:* over-generalizing the first-learned rule — this is
  precisely why 3.1.3 and 3.1.4 are sequenced back-to-back rather than 3.1.4 being
  deferred.
- **Teacher response:** "Which double are we near, and is our number bigger or smaller
  than the double's matching part?"
- **Check strategy:** commutativity.
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly chooses +1 or −1 appropriately across 3 mixed
  near-double problems.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.5.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`, `EquationWorkspace`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 3.1.5 — Halving as Undoing Doubling
- **Type / Difficulty:** Connection · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** halving an even number is the inverse of doubling — if double 6 is
  12, then half of 12 is 6.
- **Student question:** "If doubling 6 gives 12, what should half of 12 give back?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.2 (doubles fluent).
- **New idea:** halving explicitly framed as doubling's inverse operation, not a new,
  unrelated skill.
- **Reuses:** doubles facts (3.1.2), inverse-operation checking (already used
  informally in 1.3.4/1.1.4).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Inverse Operations.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` (the double's bond, read in reverse).
- **Vocabulary:** half.
- **SETUP:** the known fact `6 + 6 = 12`, already on display.
- **SEE:** "If 6 and 6 make 12, what is half of 12?"
- **BREAK:** read the bond backward: 12 splits into two *equal* parts.
- **BUILD:** state "half of 12 is 6" using the already-known double.
- **TRANSFORM:** `6 + 6 = 12` and `half of 12 = 6` — the same relationship, read in
  opposite directions.
- **CHECK:** double the answer (6) back to confirm it returns 12.
- **Primary example:** half of 12 is 6 (from double 6).
- **Second example:** half of 8 is 4 (from double 4).
- **Transfer example:** half of 20, using the known double-10 fact.
- **Misconception:** treats halving as an entirely separate skill to memorize fresh,
  rather than reading an already-known doubles fact backward. *Why:* has not yet
  internalized inverse operations as a general tool — worth explicitly naming the
  connection rather than assuming it transfers automatically.
- **Teacher response:** "Do you already know a doubles fact that would tell you this
  answer?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation (double the answer back).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly states half of 3 even numbers using their known
  doubles facts, and explains the inverse relationship.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.6.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 3.1.6 — When a Number Won't Split Evenly
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Secure/Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** halving an odd number does not give two whole-number equal parts —
  an honest limit of the strategy, noticed and named without introducing formal fraction
  notation.
- **Student question:** "Can this number be split into two exactly equal whole groups?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.5.
- **New idea:** recognizing when a relationship (evenly halving) breaks down, and
  describing the leftover informally, without inventing fraction procedures.
- **Reuses:** halving (3.1.5), part/whole splitting (0.2.1).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (physically attempting to split an odd group in
  half).
- **Vocabulary:** evenly, left over.
- **SETUP:** 7 counters; the learner is asked to split them into two equal groups.
- **SEE:** "Can you make the two groups exactly the same size?"
- **BREAK:** attempt the split — 3 and 3, with 1 left over that can't join either side
  evenly.
- **BUILD:** describe the result honestly: "7 splits into 3, 3, and 1 left over — it
  doesn't split evenly in half."
- **TRANSFORM:** an even number's half (a clean whole-number answer) vs. an odd number's
  attempted half (two equal groups plus a leftover) — the *value* 7 is unchanged either
  way; only whether a clean equal split exists changes.
- **CHECK:** recombine 3, 3, and 1 to confirm the total is still 7.
- **Primary example:** 7 → 3, 3, and 1 left over.
- **Second example:** 9 → 4, 4, and 1 left over.
- **Transfer example:** 11, predicted before attempting.
- **Misconception:** forces the leftover into one side anyway and calls the groups
  "equal" (e.g., 4 and 3, claimed as both "half of 7"). *Why:* strong pull toward
  wanting the halving procedure to always "work" — this lesson exists specifically to
  make the leftover impossible to ignore, and previews (without teaching) the idea a
  remainder later becomes a fraction (D6.3, a future release).
- **Teacher response:** "Are those two groups really the exact same size? What's left
  over?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (recombine to confirm the total).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly identifies whether a number ≤12 splits evenly in half,
  and if not, states the leftover, for 3 numbers.
- **Unlocks:** 3.1.7; plants the seed for D6.3 (Remainders as Quantities) without
  teaching it.
- **Visual:** physical objects — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 3.1.7 — Doubling Bigger Numbers
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** double a two-digit number by doubling the tens and the ones
  separately, then recombining — connects this unit back to Unit 2.1's place value.
- **Student question:** "Can you double the tens and the ones separately, and put them
  back together?"
- **Prerequisites:** 3.1.1–3.1.5, Unit 2.1 (tens and ones).
- **New idea:** distributing doubling across place value — an early, concrete instance
  of the Distribute pattern, well before D5 formalizes it for multiplication generally.
- **Reuses:** doubles facts (3.1.2), tens/ones decomposition (2.1.2/2.1.4).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Double/Half, Distribute, Break by Place Value.
- **Representation:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (expanded form) → doubling each part.
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce double, tens, ones).
- **SETUP:** `double 23`, unsolved.
- **SEE:** "23 is 2 tens and 3 ones — can we double each part separately?"
- **BREAK:** `23 = 20 + 3`.
- **BUILD:** double each part: `20 doubled = 40`; `3 doubled = 6`.
- **TRANSFORM:** `40 + 6 = 46` — same value as double 23, reached by doubling the parts
  and recombining.
- **CHECK:** add 23 + 23 directly (or via Unit 3.3's future strategies) to confirm 46.
- **Primary example:** double 23 = 46.
- **Second example:** double 14 = 28.
- **Transfer example:** double 38 — deliberately chosen because doubling the ones (16)
  crosses a ten, foreshadowing (without formally teaching) the regrouping work of
  Foundation Release 2's D3.3.
- **Misconception:** doubles only one of the two place-value parts and forgets the
  other (e.g., doubles the tens but leaves the ones unchanged). *Why:* a multi-step
  tracking error, the same family as 2.1.6's "forgot the existing tens" error.
- **Teacher response:** "You broke the number into two parts — did you double both of
  them?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation / inverse operation (direct addition
  check).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly doubles 2 two-digit numbers by doubling tens and ones
  separately and recombining.
- **Unlocks:** the Unit 3.1 Mastery Check; Unit 3.2 (Compensation) and, later, D5
  (Multiplicative Reasoning), which formalizes this distributing idea generally.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.
