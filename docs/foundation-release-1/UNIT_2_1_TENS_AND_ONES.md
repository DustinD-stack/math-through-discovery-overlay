# Unit 2.1 — Tens and Ones

- **Unit:** 2.1
- **Domain:** D2 — Place Value & Base Ten
- **Purpose:** build the conceptual foundation of place value — that a group of ten can
  be treated as one new unit ("a ten"), and that a two-digit number names how many tens
  and how many extra ones remain.
- **Prerequisites:** Unit 1.3 complete ("a ten" is already a familiar, meaningful
  quantity from Make 10); Unit 0.2 (multiple decompositions).
- **Unlocks:** the rest of D2 (Foundation Release 2), D3.3 (multi-digit strategies), D6
  (division by decomposition).
- **Primary patterns:** Unitize, Break by Place Value, Regroup/Rename.
- **Primary representations:** bundles of ten objects → `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Lesson count:** 7 instructional lessons (+ 1 unit mastery check).

---

### 2.1.1 — Ten Ones Make a New Unit
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry
- **Teaching goal:** ten individual ones can be bundled and treated as a single new
  unit — "one ten" — the conceptual heart of place value (unitizing).
- **Student question:** "If I bundle these, what do I have now?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.2.3/1.3 (10 as a familiar, complete quantity).
- **New idea:** unitizing — a group of ten counted as *one* thing, not ten things.
- **Reuses:** counting (0.1.1), the quantity 10 (1.2.3).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Unitize.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (loose ones bundled into a rod/stick of ten).
- **Vocabulary:** bundle, ten, one ten.
- **SETUP:** 10 loose counters, counted individually to confirm there are 10.
- **SEE:** "We counted 10 loose ones. What if we bundle them together?"
- **BREAK:** physically bundle the 10 ones into a single rod/bundle.
- **BUILD:** name the bundle: "this is one ten."
- **TRANSFORM:** 10 loose ones → 1 bundle of ten — same value (10), a new countable unit.
- **CHECK:** unbundle and recount the loose ones to confirm there are still 10.
- **Primary example:** 10 loose counters → 1 bundle.
- **Second example:** a second group of 10 bundled the same way, compared to the first
  (same-size bundles).
- **Transfer example:** given 2 separate bundles of ten (already made), state the total
  without unbundling (20) — first hint of place value's efficiency.
- **Misconception:** believes bundling changes the amount (fewer things now, "just one
  bundle instead of ten"). *Why:* the same conservation-of-quantity misconception from
  0.1.3, now applied to unitizing specifically — expected to resurface here and worth
  explicitly re-addressing.
- **Teacher response:** "Did we add or take away any counters when we bundled them?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (unbundle, recount).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly states a bundle of ten still represents 10 ones, and
  unbundles/recounts to confirm without prompting.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.2.
- **Visual:** bundled objects — precursor to `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 2.1.2 — Tens and Ones Names
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Developing
- **Teaching goal:** a two-digit number names how many bundles of ten and how many loose
  ones remain, and this is read directly from the number itself.
- **Student question:** "How many tens, and how many extra ones?"
- **Prerequisites:** 2.1.1.
- **New idea:** reading tens and ones directly from a two-digit numeral.
- **Reuses:** bundling (2.1.1).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Unitize.
- **Representation:** Object/Picture (bundles + loose ones) → `PlaceValueBreakdown`
  (form: columns).
- **Vocabulary:** tens, ones, digit.
- **SETUP:** 3 bundles of ten and 4 loose ones are shown together.
- **SEE:** "How many bundles? How many loose ones?"
- **BREAK:** count the bundles separately from the loose ones.
- **BUILD:** name the total using both counts: "3 tens and 4 ones."
- **TRANSFORM:** the same physical quantity, named as "34" or as "3 tens and 4 ones" —
  same value, symbol and spoken form both introduced together.
- **CHECK:** unbundle everything and count all the loose ones individually to confirm 34.
- **Primary example:** 3 tens, 4 ones → 34.
- **Second example:** 2 tens, 7 ones → 27.
- **Transfer example:** given the numeral 41 (no objects shown), predict how many
  bundles and loose ones it should be, then build it to check.
- **Misconception:** reads the digits' *face values* without connecting them to
  quantity (e.g., treats "34" as "the digit 3, then the digit 4," unable to say which
  digit represents the bundles). *Why:* numeral literacy (reading the symbol) can outrun
  place-value understanding (what the symbol means) — this is the "place-value
  digit/value confusion" flagged in `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`.
- **Teacher response:** "Which part of '34' tells us the bundles? Which part tells us
  the loose ones?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (build with bundles, then recount all
  loose).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly states the number of tens and ones for 3 different
  two-digit numbers, both from objects and from a written numeral.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.3.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (columns form).
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 2.1.3 — Same Number, Different Groupings
- **Type / Difficulty:** Connection · Developing/Secure
- **Teaching goal:** the same quantity can be grouped into tens and ones in more than one
  valid way (e.g., 34 as 3 tens 4 ones, or 2 tens 14 ones) — a direct, higher-level
  return of "same value, different form" (Unit 0.2.2/0.2.4, now at the place-value
  level).
- **Student question:** "Is there another way to group the same amount into tens and
  ones?"
- **Prerequisites:** 2.1.2, 0.2.4 (multiple decompositions).
- **New idea:** non-canonical (ungrouped/regrouped) tens-and-ones representations of the
  same value.
- **Reuses:** 0.2.4 directly, applied at a new scale.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Equivalence, Regroup/Rename.
- **Representation:** Object/Picture (bundles + loose ones, deliberately un-bundling one
  ten) → `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Vocabulary:** regroup, another way.
- **SETUP:** 34 shown as 3 tens and 4 ones (canonical form).
- **SEE:** "Is 3 tens and 4 ones the *only* way to make 34 with tens and ones?"
- **BREAK:** unbundle one ten back into 10 loose ones.
- **BUILD:** recount: now there are 2 tens and 14 ones — still 34.
- **TRANSFORM:** 34 as "3 tens, 4 ones" *or* "2 tens, 14 ones" — same value, two valid
  place-value forms (this is deliberately the same reasoning as Unit 0.2's multiple
  decompositions, now revisited in a new representation — not new content).
- **CHECK:** count all loose ones from the unbundled version — still 34.
- **Primary example:** 34 = 3 tens 4 ones = 2 tens 14 ones.
- **Second example:** 52 = 5 tens 2 ones = 4 tens 12 ones.
- **Transfer example:** the learner is given a non-canonical form (2 tens, 15 ones) and
  asked to also state the canonical form (3 tens, 5 ones) — the reverse direction.
- **Misconception:** believes 34 written as "2 tens 14 ones" is a *different number* from
  34 written as "3 tens 4 ones." *Why:* the same value/form confusion as 0.2.2,
  resurfacing at the place-value level — expected, and directly why this lesson exists
  before regrouping is needed for real work in D3.3 (Foundation Release 2).
- **Teacher response:** "Did we add or remove any ones when we unbundled — or just
  looked at the same amount differently?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (total loose ones, either way).
- **Mastery evidence:** produces both a canonical and a regrouped form for the same
  two-digit number and confirms both total the same via recount.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.4; directly prepares for D3.3's "regrouping" in Foundation Release 2.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 2.1.4 — Expanded Form
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** write a two-digit number as the sum of its place values (e.g.,
  `34 = 30 + 4`), including the honest zero-ones case (`40 = 40`, never `40 + 0`).
- **Student question:** "Can you write this number as tens plus ones added together?"
- **Prerequisites:** 2.1.2.
- **New idea:** expanded form as the symbolic version of "tens and ones" — and the
  explicit rule that a zero place is *not* written as a fake `+ 0` addend.
- **Reuses:** tens/ones naming (2.1.2), zero as a quantity (0.2.5).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Break by Place Value.
- **Representation:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (form: expanded, and form: both).
- **Vocabulary:** expanded form.
- **SETUP:** 34 (3 tens, 4 ones, already established).
- **SEE:** "What is the *value* of the 3 (thirty), and the value of the 4 (four)?"
- **BREAK:** state each digit's actual value: 3 tens = 30; 4 ones = 4.
- **BUILD:** write `34 = 30 + 4`.
- **TRANSFORM:** `34`, `3 tens and 4 ones`, and `30 + 4` — three forms, one value.
- **CHECK:** add `30 + 4` back together to confirm it returns 34.
- **Primary example:** `34 = 30 + 4`.
- **Second example:** `40 = 40` — the deliberate zero-ones case: since the ones place is
  0, expanded form has only one addend, **never** `40 + 0` (matches
  `PlaceValueBreakdown`'s production behavior exactly, and directly rehearses the D2
  `790 = 700 + 90` case coming in Foundation Release 2).
- **Transfer example:** `27 = 20 + 7`, produced independently.
- **Misconception:** writes `40 = 40 + 0` out of habit from seeing two-addend expanded
  forms in every prior example. *Why:* pattern-matching the *shape* of expanded form
  (always two addends) rather than reasoning about what each place's actual value is —
  this is exactly the "zero ones confusion" flagged in `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`, and the
  reason this lesson deliberately includes 40 as its second example rather than deferring
  the zero case to later practice.
- **Teacher response:** "What is the value of the ones place here? If it's zero, do we
  need to write anything for it?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation (add the expanded form back together).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly writes expanded form for 3 numbers including at least
  one with zero ones, without a fake `+ 0`.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.5; directly, the D2.2/D6.2 "790 = 700 + 90" reasoning in later releases.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (expanded / both).
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 2.1.5 — Comparing Two-Digit Numbers
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** compare two two-digit numbers by comparing tens first, then ones
  only if the tens are equal.
- **Student question:** "Which number is bigger — how can you tell without counting
  everything?"
- **Prerequisites:** 2.1.2, 0.1.5 (more/less/equal).
- **New idea:** an efficient comparison procedure grounded in place value, replacing
  full recounting.
- **Reuses:** more/less/equal (0.1.5), tens/ones naming (2.1.2).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Break by Place Value.
- **Representation:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (columns, two numbers side by side).
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce more/less), compare.
- **SETUP:** two numbers, e.g., 34 and 27, each shown as bundles + loose ones.
- **SEE:** "Which has more tens?"
- **BREAK:** compare the tens digits first; only compare ones if tens are tied.
- **BUILD:** state the comparison (34 > 27, because 3 tens beats 2 tens, regardless of
  ones).
- **TRANSFORM:** the same two quantities, compared efficiently by place value instead of
  by a full one-by-one count — same comparison, a faster method.
- **CHECK:** fully unbundle and count both to confirm the comparison.
- **Primary example:** 34 vs. 27.
- **Second example:** 34 vs. 31 (tens tied — ones decide).
- **Transfer example:** 34 vs. 34 (equal — both tens and ones tied).
- **Misconception:** compares the ones digit first, or compares digit-by-digit without
  regard to place (e.g., says 27 > 34 because "7 is bigger than 3 and 4"). *Why:*
  treating digits as independent numbers rather than place-valued quantities — a serious
  and common error, worth a dedicated diagnostic question.
- **Teacher response:** "Which digit tells us about tens? Doesn't a ten always count for
  more than any number of extra ones alone?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (full unbundled count confirms).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly compares 3 pairs of two-digit numbers, including one
  tied-tens case, using tens-first reasoning.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.6.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 2.1.6 — Building a Ten From Ones
- **Type / Difficulty:** Connection · Secure/Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** connect Unit 1.3's Make 10 bridge (within 20) to place value — when
  ones reach 10, they *become* a new ten, linking the addition strategy to the base-ten
  system it depends on.
- **Student question:** "You made a ten while adding before — what happens to it now
  that we're using tens and ones?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.3 (Make 10), 2.1.1 (unitizing).
- **New idea:** none new procedurally — this lesson makes explicit the connection
  between Unit 1.3's "10" and Unit 2.1's "1 ten," which up to now have been used
  side-by-side without being directly linked.
- **Reuses:** Make 10 bridge (1.3), unitizing (2.1.1).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Unitize, Make a Benchmark.
- **Representation:** Object/Picture → `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce ten, bundle).
- **SETUP:** 2 tens and 7 ones, with 5 more loose ones being added (mirrors a Unit 1.3
  bridge: 7 + 5).
- **SEE:** "We have 7 loose ones, and we're adding 5 more. What happened last time this
  came up?"
- **BREAK:** bridge the 7 ones to 10 using the familiar split (`5 = 3 + 2`).
- **BUILD:** the 10 ones formed this way get bundled into a brand-new ten.
- **TRANSFORM:** 2 tens + (7 ones bridged to a new ten + 2 leftover ones) → 3 tens and 2
  ones (32) — the Make 10 strategy and the tens/ones system, shown as one connected
  idea rather than two separate topics.
- **CHECK:** unbundle everything and count all loose ones to confirm 32.
- **Primary example:** 27 + 5 = 32, using the bridge-and-bundle connection.
- **Second example:** 38 + 6 = 44.
- **Transfer example:** 49 + 3 = 52 (crossing not just to a new ten but into a new
  "tens" digit territory — a deliberately chosen edge case).
- **Misconception:** bridges the ones correctly to 10 but forgets to bundle the new ten
  into the tens count, reporting only the leftover ones as the answer (e.g., answers "2"
  instead of "32" for 27 + 5). *Why:* loses track of the already-existing tens while
  focused on the ones-place bridge — a legitimate multi-step tracking error, not a
  conceptual one.
- **Teacher response:** "We already had some tens before we even started — what happened
  to those?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (unbundle everything).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly solves 2 two-digit-plus-single-digit bridging problems
  and reports the full two-digit answer.
- **Unlocks:** 2.1.7; this is also the lesson that satisfies the Unit 1.3 sequencing
  note's deferred two-digit Make-10 connection.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown`, `NumberBond` (for the ones-place split).
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 2.1.7 — Place Value Scavenger Hunt
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** apply tens-and-ones reasoning (naming, expanded form, comparison) to
  numbers and contexts not seen in this unit's worked examples.
- **Student question:** "What can you tell me about this number using everything you've
  learned?"
- **Prerequisites:** 2.1.1–2.1.6.
- **New idea:** none — deliberate transfer/consolidation lesson before the unit mastery
  check.
- **Reuses:** the entire unit.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Unitize, Break by Place Value.
- **Representation:** `PlaceValueBreakdown` (both form).
- **Vocabulary:** (review of the unit's vocabulary).
- **SETUP:** several two-digit numbers drawn from real objects around the room
  (classroom counts, a page number, a clock reading's minute value, etc.).
- **SEE:** "Pick a number. What do you already know how to say about it?"
- **BREAK:** the learner selects which tool(s) to apply — naming tens/ones, expanded
  form, or comparison to another found number.
- **BUILD:** state at least two facts about the chosen number using different tools.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same number, described in at least two different valid forms
  (canonical tens/ones, expanded form, or a regrouped form from 2.1.3).
- **CHECK:** a peer or teacher verifies by unbundling/counting.
- **Primary example:** a number the learner finds themselves (e.g., "23 books on this
  shelf").
- **Second example:** comparing two found numbers using 2.1.5's method.
- **Transfer example:** a number outside the unit's typical practice range (e.g., in the
  50s or 60s), applying the same tools without new instruction.
- **Misconception:** freezes when the number wasn't pre-selected by the teacher, unsure
  which tool to apply first. *Why:* prior lessons cued which specific skill to use;
  self-directed tool selection is a genuinely harder, and valuable, transfer demand.
- **Teacher response:** "What's the first thing you usually ask about a number like
  this?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (unbundle/count to verify any claim made).
- **Mastery evidence:** independently produces at least two valid, correct statements
  (naming, expanded form, or comparison) about two different self-selected numbers.
- **Unlocks:** the Unit 2.1 Mastery Check; Unit 3.1 (Doubles & Halves), which will apply
  this same place-value flexibility to two-digit doubling.
- **Visual:** `PlaceValueBreakdown`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.
