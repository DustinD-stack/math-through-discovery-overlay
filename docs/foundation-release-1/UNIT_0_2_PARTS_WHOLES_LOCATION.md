# Unit 0.2 — Parts, Wholes & Number Location

- **Unit:** 0.2
- **Domain:** D0 — Pre-Number Foundations
- **Purpose:** build compose/decompose and part/whole understanding with objects, treat
  zero as a real quantity, and introduce number location/distance and the benchmarks 5
  and 10 — all *before* the Number Bond diagram is introduced (Unit 1.1).
- **Prerequisites:** Unit 0.1 complete (secure counting, comparison, conservation of quantity).
- **Unlocks:** Unit 1.1 (Number Bonds — a model of what this unit already establishes
  concretely); Unit 1.2/1.3 (benchmarks 5 and 10).
- **Primary patterns:** Part/Whole, Unitize, Equivalence (informal).
- **Primary representations:** objects, informal bar/line drawings. No `NumberBond`
  notation yet — deliberately deferred to Unit 1.1 per P2.6.
- **Lesson count:** 8 instructional lessons (+ 1 unit mastery check).

---

### 0.2.1 — A Whole Has Parts Inside It
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry
- **Teaching goal:** a single quantity (a "whole") can be seen as made of smaller
  quantities (its "parts") without changing how many there are in total.
- **Student question:** "Can you find two smaller groups inside this one?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.1.6 (proving equality), 0.1.3 (conservation).
- **New idea:** part/whole — a whole physically split into two visible parts.
- **Reuses:** conservation of quantity (0.1.3).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (a single group, then physically separated into
  two groups by a line/gap).
- **Vocabulary:** whole, part, inside.
- **SETUP:** 7 counters in one group, counted together.
- **SEE:** "This whole group is 7. What if I slide some of them apart?"
- **BREAK:** physically slide the group into two visibly separate parts (e.g., 5 and 2).
- **BUILD:** count each part; notice they're still sitting inside what used to be "the 7."
- **TRANSFORM:** same 7 → now seen as two parts instead of one whole.
- **CHECK:** push the two parts back together and recount — still 7.
- **Primary example:** 7 split into 5 and 2.
- **Second example:** 6 split into 4 and 2.
- **Transfer example:** a group the learner splits themselves, in a way not yet shown.
- **Misconception:** believes splitting the group changes the total ("it's less now
  because it's two groups"). *Why:* directly related to 0.1.3's conservation
  misconception, now applied to a deliberate split instead of a spread.
- **Teacher response:** "Did we add any or take any away when we slid them apart?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (push back together, recount).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly predicts the whole is unchanged after a physical split,
  across two examples.
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.2 (naming this as "breaking apart" and repeating it).
- **Visual:** physical objects — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 0.2.2 — Breaking a Quantity Apart
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry/Developing
- **Teaching goal:** a quantity can be broken apart in more than one way, and each way
  still gives back the same whole.
- **Student question:** "Can you break this apart a different way?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.1.
- **New idea:** more than one decomposition exists for the same whole.
- **Reuses:** part/whole (0.2.1).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole, Equivalence.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture.
- **Vocabulary:** break apart, a different way.
- **SETUP:** 6 counters, already split one way (4 and 2) from a prior demonstration.
- **SEE:** "Is 4 and 2 the only way to break 6 apart?"
- **BREAK:** the learner re-splits the same 6 a different way (e.g., 3 and 3, or 5 and 1).
- **BUILD:** record each split found, side by side.
- **TRANSFORM:** several different-looking splits, all of the same whole (6) — the
  earliest concrete instance of "many forms, one value."
- **CHECK:** recombine each split and recount to confirm it's still 6 every time.
- **Primary example:** 6 → {4,2}, {3,3}, {5,1}.
- **Second example:** 8 → at least two different splits.
- **Transfer example:** a number the learner has not yet split (e.g., 9), find as many
  splits as they can.
- **Misconception:** treats each split as a "different number" rather than a different
  way to see the same 6. *Why:* not yet distinguishing value from form — the exact
  target concept of this lesson, expected to still be shaky here.
- **Teacher response:** "How many counters do you have altogether, no matter how you
  split them?"
- **Check strategy:** recount (recombine, recount).
- **Mastery evidence:** produces at least two different splits of the same whole and
  confirms both recombine to the same total.
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.4 (many ways to break the same number, extended).
- **Visual:** physical objects — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 0.2.3 — Putting Quantities Back Together
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Developing
- **Teaching goal:** composing (joining two known parts) is the inverse of decomposing —
  given two parts, the whole can be found by joining them.
- **Student question:** "If you know both parts, can you tell me the whole without
  counting everything again?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.1, 0.2.2.
- **New idea:** composing as the reverse of decomposing.
- **Reuses:** counting on (0.1.7), part/whole (0.2.1–0.2.2).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture.
- **Vocabulary:** put together, join, whole.
- **SETUP:** two separate small groups are shown (e.g., 3 and 4), not yet combined.
- **SEE:** "Here are two parts. What do you think the whole will be?"
- **BREAK:** predict the whole before combining (using counting-on if possible).
- **BUILD:** physically join the two groups.
- **TRANSFORM:** two separate parts → one combined whole — the reverse direction of
  0.2.1/0.2.2's split.
- **CHECK:** count the combined group to confirm the prediction.
- **Primary example:** 3 and 4 → 7.
- **Second example:** 5 and 2 → 7 (same whole as the primary example, different parts —
  connects back to 0.2.2's multiple-decomposition idea).
- **Transfer example:** three parts joined in sequence (e.g., 2, 3, and 1).
- **Misconception:** recounts the whole from 1 even after correctly predicting it via
  counting-on. *Why:* trusts counting more than reasoning at this stage — not wrong, just
  an efficiency habit still forming (mirrors 0.1.7's misconception).
- **Teacher response:** "You predicted 7 by counting on — let's check if you were right."
- **Check strategy:** recount.
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly predicts a whole from two known parts (using
  counting-on or a known fact) and confirms it, twice.
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.4.
- **Visual:** physical objects — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended.

### 0.2.4 — Many Ways to Break the Same Number
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Developing/Secure
- **Teaching goal:** systematically find *all* the whole-number ways to split a given
  small quantity, not just one or two.
- **Student question:** "Have you found every way, or might there be more?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.2, 0.2.3.
- **New idea:** exhaustive (systematic) search for decompositions, not just "a few
  examples."
- **Reuses:** 0.2.2's multiple-decomposition idea.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (an organized list/table of splits).
- **Vocabulary:** every way, in order.
- **SETUP:** the number 7 (deliberately chosen — matches the P1 example: 7 = 5+2, 4+3, 6+1).
- **SEE:** "How many different ways do you think 7 can be broken into two parts?"
- **BREAK:** search systematically — start from one part being 1, then 2, then 3, and so
  on, to avoid missing or repeating a split.
- **BUILD:** record each pair found (1&6, 2&5, 3&4) and notice when pairs start
  repeating in reverse (4&3 is 3&4 again).
- **TRANSFORM:** the same whole (7), organized as a complete, ordered list of forms.
- **CHECK:** count the total number of *distinct* splits found and justify none were
  missed or duplicated.
- **Primary example:** 7 = 6+1, 5+2, 4+3 (and their reverses).
- **Second example:** 6 = 5+1, 4+2, 3+3.
- **Transfer example:** 9, found independently by the learner using the same systematic
  method.
- **Misconception:** lists 2&5 and 5&2 as two different splits. *Why:* hasn't yet
  connected this to 0.1's comparison work — order of the parts doesn't change the whole,
  but this is a legitimate open question at this stage, not corrected harshly.
- **Teacher response:** "Is 5 and 2 a different amount from 2 and 5, or just written in a
  different order?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (physically swap the two groups' positions
  — same whole).
- **Mastery evidence:** finds at least 3 distinct splits of 7 (or 6) using a systematic
  method, not trial and error.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.6 (Many Bonds for the Same Number) directly extends this into bond notation.
- **Visual:** physical objects / an organized picture list — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 0.2.5 — Zero Is Still a Quantity
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Developing
- **Teaching goal:** zero is a legitimate answer to "how many" (an empty group), not "no
  answer" or "nothing to talk about."
- **Student question:** "If I take all of them away, how many are left? Is that still an
  amount?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.1.
- **New idea:** zero as a real, nameable quantity — including as a legitimate *part* of
  a whole.
- **Reuses:** part/whole (0.2.1).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (an empty tray/space, explicitly shown as "a
  group with zero in it," not just absence).
- **Vocabulary:** zero, none, empty.
- **SETUP:** 5 counters in a tray; one at a time, all 5 are removed.
- **SEE:** "How many are in the tray now?"
- **BREAK:** name each amount as objects are removed: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 ...
- **BUILD:** name the final amount — zero — as a real answer, not "nothing left to
  count."
- **TRANSFORM:** the tray, full → empty, is still a tray with a countable amount in it
  (zero) at every step — same kind of question, different (eventually zero) answer.
- **CHECK:** put one counter back — is the tray still "zero," or something else now?
  (confirms zero is a specific, meaningful value, not a permanent label.)
- **Primary example:** 5 counters removed one at a time to 0.
- **Second example:** 3 = 3 + 0 and 3 = 0 + 3 as valid splits of 3 (connects to 0.2.4's
  decomposition work — zero is a legitimate part).
- **Transfer example:** "How many elephants are in this room?" — zero in a context with
  no physical removal to demonstrate.
- **Misconception:** says "there's no number" or "there's nothing" instead of "zero."
  *Why:* treating the *absence* of objects as the absence of a quantity, rather than as
  the specific quantity zero — this directly seeds the D2 "zero ones" issue in
  `PlaceValueBreakdown` (790 = 700 + 90, no fake `+0`), so it is worth getting right here.
- **Teacher response:** "Is that the same as not being able to answer the question, or is
  zero itself the answer?"
- **Check strategy:** mental reasonableness (zero as an answer that still answers "how
  many").
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly states "zero" as the count of an empty group in at
  least two different contexts, including one with no physical objects at all.
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.6 (number location — zero as a specific point, not "off the line").
- **Visual:** an empty tray / empty picture frame — no MTD component.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended (concrete removal reads well on camera).

### 0.2.6 — Where Do Numbers Live?
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** numbers have a location relative to each other — they can be placed
  in order along a path, and that placement itself carries meaning.
- **Student question:** "Where would this number go?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.1.5 (more/less/equal), 0.2.5 (zero as a quantity).
- **New idea:** number location — the seed of the number line, used from here through
  algebra.
- **Reuses:** more/less/equal (0.1.5), zero (0.2.5).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole (ordering as an extension of comparison).
- **Representation:** Picture — an informal path/line with markers, not yet a ruled
  `NumberLine` component.
- **Vocabulary:** before, after, between, order.
- **SETUP:** a blank path with a start point (zero) marked.
- **SEE:** "If this end is zero, where do you think 3 belongs?"
- **BREAK:** place a few numbers (0, 1, 2, 3) one at a time, checking each against
  "more than the last one, so further along."
- **BUILD:** fill in a short run of consecutive numbers on the path.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same numbers, shown as a path of *positions* instead of as separate
  counted groups — same values, a new (spatial) form.
- **CHECK:** does the order along the path match "more is farther from zero"?
- **Primary example:** placing 0–5 on a path.
- **Second example:** inserting 6 and 7 onto an already-placed 0–5 path.
- **Transfer example:** given two already-placed numbers (e.g., 2 and 6), predict roughly
  where 4 belongs between them.
- **Misconception:** places numbers by size of the written numeral rather than by
  quantity order (e.g., unsure whether 2 or 10 is "bigger" on the path because "10 has
  two digits, that's confusing"). *Why:* numeral form and quantity are still being
  connected — flags a place to double back if it persists into D2.
- **Teacher response:** "Which amount is more — 2 counters or 10 counters? Where should
  the bigger amount go on our path?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (compare quantities with objects, then
  re-check the path placement).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly places 4–5 given numbers in order along a path and
  justifies "more is farther along."
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.7 (distance between placed numbers).
- **Visual:** an informal number path — early precursor to `NumberLine`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 0.2.7 — How Far Apart?
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** the distance between two quantities/positions is itself a quantity
  that can be found and named — plants the seed for subtraction-as-distance.
- **Student question:** "How far apart are these two numbers?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.6.
- **New idea:** distance between two quantities as a nameable amount.
- **Reuses:** number location (0.2.6), counting on (0.1.7).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole (distance as a "gap" part).
- **Representation:** Picture — the informal number path with two points marked and the
  gap between them highlighted.
- **Vocabulary:** how far, gap, apart.
- **SETUP:** two numbers marked on the path from 0.2.6 (e.g., 3 and 7).
- **SEE:** "How far do you have to travel from 3 to get to 7?"
- **BREAK:** count the steps along the path from one mark to the other.
- **BUILD:** name the distance (4 steps) as its own quantity.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same gap, described either as "steps from 3 to 7" or "steps from 7
  back to 3" — same distance, different direction/form.
- **CHECK:** count the steps the other direction (7 back to 3) — same distance.
- **Primary example:** distance from 3 to 7.
- **Second example:** distance from 2 to 9.
- **Transfer example:** which of two given pairs of numbers is farther apart?
- **Misconception:** counts the *starting* number itself as one of the steps (off-by-one
  error, e.g., counts 3,4,5,6,7 as 5 steps instead of 4). *Why:* counting positions
  instead of counting the moves/gaps between them — a very common and durable error worth
  flagging into `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`.
- **Teacher response:** "Let's count the jumps between the numbers, not the numbers
  themselves — how many jumps did it take?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (count the other direction; should match).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly finds the distance between two numbers ≤10 without an
  off-by-one error, twice.
- **Unlocks:** 0.2.8 (5 and 10 as benchmark distances worth knowing well); later, D6's
  number-line CHECK strategy.
- **Visual:** informal number path — precursor to `NumberLine`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 0.2.8 — Five and Ten: Numbers Worth Knowing Well
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Secure/Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** 5 and 10 are useful "landmark" quantities — many other quantities
  can be understood in relation to how far they are from 5 or from 10.
- **Student question:** "Is this number close to 5? Close to 10? How do you know?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.6, 0.2.7.
- **New idea:** benchmark quantities — the direct prerequisite for Unit 1.2's Make-5/
  Make-10 complement work.
- **Reuses:** number location (0.2.6), distance (0.2.7).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Make a Benchmark (earliest, fully informal instance).
- **Representation:** Object → Picture (a ten-frame introduced here for the first time,
  as a landmark tool, not yet an addition strategy).
- **Vocabulary:** close to, landmark, benchmark.
- **SETUP:** a ten-frame with several dots filled in (e.g., 7 filled).
- **SEE:** "How many are filled in? How many empty spaces are left?"
- **BREAK:** separate "how many filled" from "how many empty" within the same ten-frame.
- **BUILD:** state both: "7 filled, 3 empty" — the empty spaces are the distance to 10.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same quantity (7), described as "7," or as "3 away from 10" — same
  value, benchmark-relative form.
- **CHECK:** fill in the empty spaces and count all 10 to confirm 7 + 3 = 10.
- **Primary example:** 7 on a ten-frame (3 away from 10).
- **Second example:** 8 on a ten-frame (2 away from 10).
- **Transfer example:** a quantity near 5 instead of 10 (e.g., 4 on a five-frame, 1 away
  from 5).
- **Misconception:** correctly counts the filled spaces but cannot state the empty count
  without recounting one at a time. *Why:* has not yet connected "filled + empty = 10"
  as a fixed relationship — expected here, and the direct target of Unit 1.2.
- **Teacher response:** "We know the frame holds 10 in all. If 7 are filled, must you
  count the empty ones, or can you figure it out?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation / recount (fill the gaps, recount to 10).
- **Mastery evidence:** given a ten-frame with 6–9 filled, correctly states the number of
  empty spaces without recounting them individually, twice.
- **Unlocks:** the Unit 0.2 Mastery Check; Unit 1.2 (Benchmark Numbers) directly.
- **Visual:** ten-frame — no MTD component yet (a strong future-component candidate,
  flagged in `README.md`).
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.
