# Unit 1.1 — Number Bonds

- **Unit:** 1.1
- **Domain:** D1 — Number Sense & Composition
- **Purpose:** introduce the Number Bond diagram as a **model** of the part/whole
  understanding already built in Unit 0.2 — not as a new diagram to memorize.
- **Prerequisites:** Unit 0.2 complete (part/whole, compose/decompose, multiple
  decompositions of a number).
- **Unlocks:** Unit 1.2 (Benchmark Numbers), Unit 1.3 (Make 10).
- **Primary patterns:** Part/Whole, Equivalence.
- **Primary representations:** objects → `NumberBond`.
- **Lesson count:** 7 instructional lessons (+ 1 unit mastery check).

---

### 1.1.1 — A Model for Parts and Whole
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry
- **Teaching goal:** the Number Bond diagram (whole on top, two parts below) is a picture
  of the splitting/joining a learner already knows how to do with objects — not a new
  rule to learn.
- **Student question:** "Can you show your object split using this picture instead?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.1, 0.2.2, 0.2.3.
- **New idea:** the `NumberBond` diagram as a *representation* of an already-understood
  action.
- **Reuses:** part/whole splitting (0.2.1–0.2.3).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** Object → Picture → `NumberBond`.
- **Vocabulary:** whole, part, bond.
- **SETUP:** 7 counters, already split into 5 and 2 as in prior lessons.
- **SEE:** "You already know 7 splits into 5 and 2. Here's a new way to draw that."
- **BREAK:** map the physical whole and parts onto the diagram's three positions.
- **BUILD:** draw/build the bond for 7 = 5 + 2 with the whole on top, parts below.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same split (7 → 5 and 2), shown as objects *or* as a bond diagram —
  same value, a new drawn form.
- **CHECK:** cover the bond's whole and rebuild it from the two parts (objects), then
  reveal — does it match?
- **Primary example:** 7 = 5 + 2 (matches the Unit 0.2 example directly).
- **Second example:** 6 = 4 + 2.
- **Transfer example:** a split the learner has already found themselves in Unit 0.2,
  redrawn as a bond without teacher demonstration.
- **Misconception:** treats the bond diagram as an unrelated new fact to memorize,
  disconnected from the objects. *Why:* the diagram is introduced too fast, before the
  mapping from concrete to picture is made explicit — this lesson exists specifically to
  prevent that by starting from a split the learner already trusts.
- **Teacher response:** "Where do your actual counters go on this picture?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (objects and bond should agree).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly draws a bond for a split already demonstrated with
  objects, twice.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.2.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Recommended (mapping objects to the diagram benefits
  from a visible presenter narrating the connection).

### 1.1.2 — Reading a Number Bond
- **Type / Difficulty:** Discovery · Entry/Developing
- **Teaching goal:** given an already-drawn bond (whole and both parts filled in), state
  what it says as a full sentence, and connect it back to objects if asked.
- **Student question:** "What does this bond tell you?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1.1.
- **New idea:** fluently *reading* a bond, independent of having just built it.
- **Reuses:** 1.1.1's mapping.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` → Object (on request, to verify).
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce whole, part, bond).
- **SETUP:** a pre-drawn, fully filled-in bond (e.g., whole 8, parts 6 and 2).
- **SEE:** "What does this picture tell us, without me saying anything?"
- **BREAK:** identify which number is the whole and which two are the parts.
- **BUILD:** state the relationship aloud ("8 is made of 6 and 2").
- **TRANSFORM:** the same bond, read starting from either part ("6 and 2 make 8" / "8 is
  6 and 2") — same relationship, either direction.
- **CHECK:** build it with objects to confirm the bond is telling the truth.
- **Primary example:** whole 8, parts 6 and 2.
- **Second example:** whole 9, parts 7 and 2.
- **Transfer example:** a bond with the parts listed in reverse position from the
  previous examples, to confirm position (left/right) doesn't change the reading.
- **Misconception:** always reads "whole first" only, and struggles to also state
  "part + part = whole" starting from the parts. *Why:* has learned one fixed reading
  order rather than the flexible relationship — worth catching early since Unit 1.1.4/
  1.1.5 depend on reading in both directions.
- **Teacher response:** "Can you also start with the two parts and tell me what they
  make?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (objects confirm the bond).
- **Mastery evidence:** reads a given bond correctly starting from the whole and starting
  from the parts, for two different bonds.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.3.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 1.1.3 — Whole Known, Parts Known
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Developing
- **Teaching goal:** build and verify a bond when both the whole and both parts are
  already known — the straightforward case, before any part is missing.
- **Student question:** "Does everything in this bond agree?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1.2.
- **New idea:** verifying a fully-known bond is internally consistent (parts add to the
  whole) — sets up recognizing when something is *missing* next.
- **Reuses:** composing (0.2.3).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond`.
- **Vocabulary:** check, agree.
- **SETUP:** a bond with the whole and both parts filled in, sometimes correct, sometimes
  deliberately wrong (e.g., whole 9, parts 5 and 3).
- **SEE:** "Every number in this bond is already filled in. Is it correct?"
- **BREAK:** add the two parts together independently of what the bond claims the whole is.
- **BUILD:** compare the computed sum to the stated whole.
- **TRANSFORM:** a "broken" bond, corrected into a true one — same numbers rearranged
  into a form that's actually consistent.
- **CHECK:** rebuild with objects if the bond is in question.
- **Primary example:** a correct bond (9 = 6+3).
- **Second example:** a deliberately incorrect bond (9 claimed, but parts are 5 and 3).
- **Transfer example:** the learner builds their own fully-known, correct bond from a
  chosen whole.
- **Misconception:** accepts an incorrect bond because "the picture looks right" without
  checking the numbers. *Why:* treating the bond as a shape to recognize rather than a
  numeric claim to verify — this lesson is specifically designed to break that habit.
- **Teacher response:** "Let's add the parts ourselves — does it match what the bond
  says the whole is?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation (parts should sum to the whole — verify by
  addition).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly identifies at least one true and one false bond, with
  justification.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.4 (missing-part reasoning follows naturally from verifying known bonds).
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 1.1.4 — Missing Part Puzzles
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Developing/Secure
- **Teaching goal:** given the whole and one part, find the missing part.
- **Student question:** "What's hiding in the empty part?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1.3.
- **New idea:** solving for a missing part (the direct precursor to "missing addend"
  reasoning used throughout arithmetic and later, algebra).
- **Reuses:** counting on (0.1.7), whole/parts relationship (1.1.2–1.1.3).
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole, Unknown as Missing Quantity (informal, earliest instance).
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` (one part position left blank).
- **Vocabulary:** missing, hidden.
- **SETUP:** a bond with the whole (e.g., 8) and one part (5) shown; the other part is
  covered/blank.
- **SEE:** "We know the whole is 8, and one part is 5. What's under the cover?"
- **BREAK:** use counting-on from 5 toward 8, or objects, to find the missing amount.
- **BUILD:** state the missing part (3) and place it in the bond.
- **TRANSFORM:** the same whole (8), now shown as a *complete* bond after solving for the
  gap — same value throughout, the unknown becomes known.
- **CHECK:** reveal the cover / build with objects to confirm 5 + 3 = 8.
- **Primary example:** whole 8, known part 5, missing part 3.
- **Second example:** whole 9, known part 6, missing part 3.
- **Transfer example:** whole 10, known part 4 — connects directly toward Unit 1.2's
  "what does 10 need" framing.
- **Misconception:** adds the known part to the whole instead of finding the difference
  (e.g., for whole 8, part 5, answers "13"). *Why:* over-applies "put together" reasoning
  to a "what's left" situation — a durable and common error, flagged in
  `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`.
- **Teacher response:** "If the whole is 8 and we already have 5, would the missing part
  be bigger or smaller than 8?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation (known part + found part should equal the whole).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly solves 3 missing-part bonds with wholes ≤10 without
  the addition-instead-of-difference error.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.5 (missing whole is the companion case).
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 1.1.5 — Missing Whole Puzzles
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** given both parts, find the whole — the companion case to 1.1.4,
  reinforcing composing.
- **Student question:** "If you know both parts, what's the whole?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1.4.
- **New idea:** none new conceptually (this is composing, 0.2.3, in bond form) — the
  lesson exists to make the *bond* version of composing fluent, distinct from the
  *decomposing* version drilled in 1.1.4.
- **Reuses:** composing (0.2.3), counting on.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` (whole position left blank).
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce whole, part).
- **SETUP:** a bond with both parts shown (e.g., 6 and 3), whole blank.
- **SEE:** "Both parts are here. What whole do they make?"
- **BREAK:** combine the two parts (counting on or a known fact).
- **BUILD:** state and place the whole (9).
- **TRANSFORM:** two separate known parts → one combined named whole.
- **CHECK:** build with objects to confirm 6 + 3 = 9.
- **Primary example:** parts 6 and 3, whole 9.
- **Second example:** parts 7 and 2, whole 9 (same whole as the primary example, via
  different parts — reinforces 0.2.4's multiple-decomposition idea from the "build"
  direction).
- **Transfer example:** three parts given at once, find the whole (extends the bond
  model beyond two parts informally).
- **Misconception:** confuses which blank is the whole vs. a part when the diagram's
  layout is unfamiliar (e.g., parts stacked vertically instead of side by side). *Why:*
  relying on diagram *position* rather than the *whole-is-always-bigger-or-equal*
  relationship — flagged as "number-bond direction confusion" in `MISCONCEPTION_MAP.md`.
- **Teacher response:** "Which number should be the biggest one here — the whole, or one
  of the parts?"
- **Check strategy:** inverse operation (parts should sum to the found whole).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly finds the whole from two given parts for 3 different
  pairs.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.6.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 1.1.6 — Many Bonds for the Same Number
- **Type / Difficulty:** Strategy · Secure
- **Teaching goal:** the same whole has multiple valid bonds — direct extension of
  Unit 0.2.4 into bond notation.
- **Student question:** "How many different bonds can you build for the same whole?"
- **Prerequisites:** 0.2.4, 1.1.3–1.1.5.
- **New idea:** none new — deliberate re-encounter of an established idea (multiple
  decompositions) in a new representation, which *is* the point (representation
  progression, not new content).
- **Reuses:** 0.2.4 directly.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole, Equivalence.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` (several drawn side by side for the same whole).
- **Vocabulary:** (reinforce many ways, same whole).
- **SETUP:** the whole 7 (same number used in 0.2.4, deliberately).
- **SEE:** "You already found every way to split 7 with objects. Can you draw them all as
  bonds?"
- **BREAK:** recall or re-derive 7's splits (6&1, 5&2, 4&3).
- **BUILD:** draw a bond for each split.
- **TRANSFORM:** three different bonds, one shared whole — same value, three drawn forms.
- **CHECK:** verify each bond's parts sum to 7.
- **Primary example:** 7 = 6+1, 5+2, 4+3 (bonds).
- **Second example:** 8's full set of splits, as bonds.
- **Transfer example:** 10's splits, as bonds — directly feeds Unit 1.2/1.3.
- **Misconception:** draws the same split twice with parts swapped, believing it's a new
  bond (e.g., both "5&2" and "2&5" as separate bonds). *Why:* same root cause as 0.2.4's
  ordering misconception, now revisited in the new representation to confirm it
  transferred (or didn't).
- **Teacher response:** "Is that a new way to split 7, or the same split with the parts
  in different spots?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (rebuild with objects; swapping part
  position doesn't change the objects).
- **Mastery evidence:** produces the complete set of distinct bonds for a whole ≤10.
- **Unlocks:** 1.1.7 (bond to equation) and, directly, Unit 1.3's Make 10 work.
- **Visual:** `NumberBond`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.

### 1.1.7 — From Bond to Equation
- **Type / Difficulty:** Connection · Secure/Transfer
- **Teaching goal:** a bond and an addition equation say the same thing in two different
  forms — the first explicit PICTURE → SYMBOL bridge in the curriculum.
- **Student question:** "Can you write what this bond says as a number sentence?"
- **Prerequisites:** 1.1.1–1.1.6.
- **New idea:** the equation `whole = part + part` as a symbolic form of the bond.
- **Reuses:** every prior lesson in this unit.
- **Reasoning pattern(s):** Part/Whole, Equivalence.
- **Representation:** `NumberBond` → `EquationWorkspace` (a single given/result line, not
  yet a multi-step flow — this lesson is the bridge into that later flow).
- **Vocabulary:** equation, number sentence, equals.
- **SETUP:** a completed bond (7 = 5 + 2).
- **SEE:** "This bond and this number sentence — do they say the same thing?"
- **BREAK:** map each part of the bond onto a piece of the equation (whole → the number
  after "="; each part → a number being added).
- **BUILD:** write the equation from the bond (7 = 5 + 2), and the bond from a given
  equation (the reverse direction).
- **TRANSFORM:** the same relationship, in picture form (bond) or symbol form (equation)
  — Object→Picture→Symbol, completed for this concept.
- **CHECK:** build the whole with objects and confirm both the bond and the equation
  describe it correctly.
- **Primary example:** 7 = 5 + 2 (bond and equation).
- **Second example:** 9 = 6 + 3 (bond and equation), reversed order (write the bond first
  from a given equation).
- **Transfer example:** a three-part decomposition (e.g., 6 = 2+2+2) expressed as an
  equation, extending past the two-part bond model.
- **Misconception:** writes the equation with the whole in the wrong position (e.g.,
  `5 + 2 = 7` is fine, but writes `7 + 5 = 2` by placing numbers in the order they were
  read off the bond, not by their role). *Why:* treating equation-writing as a
  transcription of positions on the page rather than a translation of roles (whole vs.
  part) — an important habit to catch before symbolic work becomes more complex.
- **Teacher response:** "Which number is the whole in your bond? Where does the whole go
  in the number sentence?"
- **Check strategy:** alternate representation (bond and equation should describe the
  same objects).
- **Mastery evidence:** correctly translates between a bond and an equation in both
  directions, for two different wholes.
- **Unlocks:** the Unit 1.1 Mastery Check; Unit 1.2/1.3 (benchmark and Make-10 reasoning
  is expressed symbolically from here on, atop this bridge).
- **Visual:** `NumberBond` and `EquationWorkspace`.
- **Format:** Both. **Presenter:** Optional.
